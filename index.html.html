How We Work

What we're seeking to explore

Since 2015, ASE has been experienced live — in intimate workshops, public exhibitions, wellness gatherings, and private sessions — as an intentionally composed environment of resonant frequencies, harmonic sequences, and spatial sound. People keep describing the same handful of shifts: calm, presence, altered states of mind, physical sensation, emotional release.

What USF is now seeking to explore is simple to state and hard to answer: is there something measurable happening in the brain when this occurs — and can it be studied with the same rigor given to any other candidate for medicine?

Calm. Presence. Stress response. Memory. These are not the words of a marketing brief — they're what people, unprompted, keep saying for themselves. And they are also, not coincidentally, the exact territory the Lieber Institute study investigates: emotional regulation, stress response, and associative memory.

But consistent subjective experience, however widespread, is not scientific evidence. That gap — between what people feel and what can be measured — is the reason USF exists in its current form: not to keep collecting stories, but to find out whether there's something real and repeatable underneath them.

Our First Research Partner

The Lieber Institute for Brain Development

To take these patterns seriously, USF sought a research partner equipped to study the brain with genuine scientific rigor. The Lieber Institute for Brain Development (LIBD) is now that partner — and Listening to the Brain is the first study in what USF intends to be an ongoing research relationship.

The Research

Listening to the Brain

The first study of the USF × LIBD partnership, translated here in plain terms.

01

The problem

Chronic stress, anxiety, depression, and substance use affect hundreds of millions of people worldwide. Existing treatments — medication, and newer approaches such as psychedelic‑assisted therapy — don't work for everyone, and can carry side effects, safety questions, or scalability limits.

02

The insight

The brain is fundamentally rhythmic: neurons communicate through oscillating electrical patterns, and disruptions in those rhythms are increasingly recognized as a feature of conditions like depression, anxiety, and PTSD. Sound is itself rhythmic and oscillatory, and connects directly to the limbic circuitry — including the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex — involved in emotion and memory.

03

The question

Can specific sonic frequencies and harmonic sequences be studied as a biological stimulus — not simply something that feels calming, but something that can be shown, with data, to modulate the neural systems behind emotional regulation and stress response?

04

The study

The Lieber Institute for Brain Development is designing a controlled laboratory study that compares a therapeutic harmonic sequence against a control sound and against silence, while directly measuring brain activity and behavior — a first, foundational step toward establishing whether this effect is real, measurable, and repeatable.

Each mouse is implanted with depth electrodes in the prelimbic cortex and dorsal hippocampus (CA1) — regions responsible for processing the emotional content of sound and associative learning. Using a within‑subjects crossover design, every mouse is tested under all three conditions (therapeutic harmonic sequence, control sound, no sound) in pseudorandom order, across three‑hour sessions: one hour of habituation, one hour of stimulus, and one hour of post‑stimulus observation to detect any lasting effect.

Researchers will measure local field potentials (LFPs) for changes in oscillation power and synchronization, alongside automated, machine‑learning‑based behavioral tracking of locomotor activity.

05

Why it matters

This is a proof‑of‑concept study, not a finished treatment. By directly measuring how structured sound influences brain oscillations and behavior, it moves the conversation about sonic therapeutics from belief toward biology — the first step in giving sound the same rigorous scientific scrutiny already given to any drug or device.

The Road Ahead

Bringing the ASE Method forward through science

The Lieber Institute study is the first step, not the last. USF's longer‑term goal is to keep developing the ASE Method through research and data — study by study. These are possible future directions described in the research proposal, contingent on what this first study finds, not commitments or claims about outcomes.

Identifying which frequencies and harmonic structures are most effective, and in which brain regions.
Developing non‑invasive markers (such as EEG) that could eventually inform early human research.
Exploring, in time, whether sonic interventions could reduce stress or trauma‑related symptoms in real‑world settings.
